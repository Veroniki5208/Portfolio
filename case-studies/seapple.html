Seapple: a stamped passport for Seattle's farmers markets

Designed for the UN's Life on Land sustainability goal — a stamp-rally passport that rewards farmers market visits with a polymer clay keychain and a hand-sewn plush prize, encouraging people to eat locally and learn about Washington's food systems.

Concept

Turning farmers market visits into a scavenger hunt

For this assignment, our team designed for the UN's sustainability goal Life on Land, focusing on supporting local agriculture and building knowledge around locally grown crops in the Seattle area. We modeled the concept on stamp rallies — participants visit different places to collect stamps toward a prize — applied to local farmers markets, to promote eating locally and educate people about local food systems.

Participants receive a paper manual introducing local farmers, guiding them through each market and letting them collect a stamp at each of the 8 locations. Completing all stamps earns a plush prize shaped like an apple, since Washington is a top apple producer nationally.

To build this, the team used clay to prototype the plush's form, sewing and textiles for the final plush, 3D printing for a unique stamp, paper prototyping for the manual, and a video prototype to show the full use case.

Design Goals

Three lenses the whole team designed against

1
Usability. Are people familiar with stamp-collection reward systems? Is the manual easy to understand? How much effort does collecting stamps actually take?
2
Impact. After participating, would people actually buy produce from local farmers? Do they feel they're doing something meaningful? Are they informed about local agriculture, and motivated to visit markets?
3
Desirability. Are the prizes incentivizing enough? Are people excited about the process? Would they encourage others, or bring friends and family?

Evaluation

After the initial print, the team ran usability testing including card sorting to determine what order people expect to view seasons in — winter came out first. Testing asked whether any expected information was missing, whether instructions made sense, whether people would actually use it, whether they'd keep it after finishing, and how it would feel to carry around.

Users found the general idea interesting and the information useful and clear, with an easy-to-carry size. They liked the design and cover, would keep it after finishing the stamps, and would enjoy the activity with friends. Feedback included wanting more transportation information and fixing text alignment issues.Manual usability testing summary

A digital flip-book version was also created so testing could happen without the physical copy, and it was reused in the video prototype. I built the digital flip book.

Plush & Clay Prize

From clay prototype to a two-tier reward system

A different teammate designed and prototyped the plush prize. I worked on the clay prize prototype and led usability testing for both prizes.

For the prize, the team first shaped the Seapple mascot out of polymer clay to test the form for the plush. The clay figurine was small enough that we added a keychain attachment so it wouldn't be easily lost. After user testing, we found people quite liked the standalone clay keychain — so in addition to the final plush prize, the keychain became a "halfway" prize, awarded after 4 of 8 stamps, to keep people motivated to complete the full set.

Clay apple keychain prototype
assets/images/seapple/clay-keychain.png

Evaluation

We evaluated both prizes with multiple user testers — asking how incentivizing the prizes felt on a 1-5 scale, whether the halfway keychain would sustain motivation to finish all 8 markets, and whether the prize felt fun and connected to the farmers market theme, alongside questions about materials and size.

Participants said collecting is very fun and the prizes are great incentives for visiting. Some noted that as students, they'd mainly participate in summer when they had time off. They loved the apple mascot and found it fitting for Washington, "the apple state." The plush's soft velvet material made it feel high-end and much more desirable.Prize usability testing summary
Video Prototype

Showing the full use case, start to finish

I produced the video prototype and led its user testing.

Physically visiting each market to test the experience would take too long for user testers to evaluate. Instead, we made a video prototype showing the full process: getting the manual, going to Pike Place, getting stamped, collecting the remaining stamps, and receiving the keychain (4/8) and plush (8/8) prizes. We storyboarded every scene needed before gathering props — the booklet, the stamp and ink pad, and both prizes — for filming.

Video prototype — full use case walkthrough
assets/images/seapple/video-prototype.mp4 (or link to YouTube)

Evaluation

We showed the video to 4 user testers alongside a survey covering usability, desirability, and impact — what parts of the process were confusing, how excited they were to participate (1-5 scale), whether they'd do this alone or with others, how likely they'd be to buy local produce, and whether they saw the program growing.

All participants found the prize very cute and appealing, and the stamp collection brought back childhood memories. 3 of 4 participants would bring a friend or family member. The only confusing part was exactly where participants would collect the manual, stamps, and prizes at each market — solvable with a clearly branded Seapple booth. One participant also asked who would supply the prizes at scale, which we haven't fully resolved.Video prototype survey summary

Reviewers consistently praised usability, desirability, and impact, noting the prototypes successfully motivate users and clearly support the UN's Life on Land goal by encouraging local shopping and supporting small farms. The stamp system was seen as a good and creative way to change behavior, and the apple plushie and keychains were repeatedly described as cute, effective rewards that made the experience feel like an adventure.
