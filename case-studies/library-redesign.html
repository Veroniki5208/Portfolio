Making library impact easier to understand

Redesigning UW Libraries' Quick Facts & Data page to balance user comprehension with the needs of multiple internal departments who each wanted their numbers represented.

Problem

Valuable library data was organized as a muddled, disconnected list — clear to no single audience, useful to none.

Approach

Combined a sponsor interview, a 9-point heuristic evaluation, competitor analysis, and a 12-response survey to find what was actually breaking, then rebuilt the information hierarchy around it.

Outcome / Status

Full redesigned Figma mockup delivered across 9 major page sections, reviewed by the sponsor across two rounds. A follow-up survey testing the redesign is planned this week.

Of the final design, I personally designed the Hero Section, Campus Footprint, Community Events, and Student POV sections. The Challenge

A page with real information and no coherent story

UW Libraries' Quick Facts & Data page existed to show the library's impact — usage numbers, teaching statistics, collection size, community reach — to an external audience that could include prospective donors and university stakeholders, not just students. The problem wasn't that this information didn't matter. Every number on the page came from a real department that wanted it represented.

The problem was structural: the UW Quick Facts and Data page suffered from a muddled information hierarchy, poor navigation and accessibility, and disconnected data — a conclusion we reached from two independent sources: an interview with our sponsor, Maggie Faber, and a heuristic evaluation of the existing site. Cutting content wasn't an option — every department's numbers needed to stay visible. The real design problem was building a hierarchy that could hold all of it without becoming unreadable.

Working With a Real Stakeholder

The sponsor's instinct was right — figuring out "how" took separate analysis

As team lead, I was the primary point of contact with Maggie Faber on the UW Libraries Data & Assessment Team throughout the project. Her framing of the problem — that information felt scattered "here and there" and needed better organization and clearer communication of value to an external audience — came from an early interview and directly shaped our problem statement. But a stakeholder naming a symptom isn't the same as a diagnosis: her interview told us that something was wrong; it didn't tell us exactly where or why. That's what the heuristic evaluation and survey were for.

Maggie's involvement didn't stop at the opening interview. She also provided the source materials that shaped our research — the current page content, the library's Fact Sheet, and the Tableau dashboard behind the numbers. Once we had wireframes, we brought them back to her for feedback, and she in turn shared them with members of her staff, who gave input both on the designs themselves and on what was technically feasible for IT to actually build. That feedback shaped how we consolidated multiple wireframe directions into one, which we brought back to her again before moving into hi-fi.

9-point heuristic evaluation (Nielsen Norman Group framework)

HeuristicWhat we found
Visibility of system statusStatic breadcrumbs with no scroll-depth or active-section tracking
Match with the real worldInternal library jargon and raw numbers with no real-world context
User control & freedomNo anchor links or back-to-top buttons for page navigation
Consistency & standardsInconsistent emphasis — bolding only numbers created an awkward visual hierarchy
Error preventionLow visual contrast on critical buttons (e.g., "Give to UW Libraries")
Recognition over recallRelied on text lists over charts, forcing users to recall what each number meant
Flexibility & efficiencyMissing quick export tools (PDF/print) or filters for fast data access
Aesthetic & minimalist designDense, repetitive text lists created high visual clutter and cognitive load
Help & documentation"Ask Us" support was hidden in the header instead of contextual with the data

Competitor analysis

We looked at how peer institutions (University of Oregon, Duke University, and others) handled similar impact reporting, and pulled four patterns worth adopting:

→
Time & methodological transparency. Display explicit timeframes for every data set, instead of static numbers with no defined measurement period.
→
Information architecture & mental models. Pair top-level quantitative statistics with adjacent qualitative user narratives, to accommodate both scanning and reading behavior.
→
Spatial consistency & cognitive load. Use charts instead of spreadsheets, and pair key metrics with relevant imagery for instant recognition.
→
User control. Sticky navigation, prominent export controls, and contextual support alongside complex data.
Research — Checking Our Read Against Real Visitors

12 responses, and one finding that mattered more than the rest

Access to the page's actual external audience (donors, prospective stakeholders) was limited within the project timeline, so we surveyed a smaller, more reachable group — 12 total responses. This is directional, not representative: 10 respondents were current UW students, 1 an incoming student, and 1 admin staff (6 viewed on mobile, 5 desktop, 1 laptop); 10 of the 12 were first-time visitors to the page.

→
Low clarity. 2 of 12 respondents rated the page's purpose as unclear, describing it as an aimless list of facts.
→
Most memorable sections. Community Events and Statistics stood out as the most memorable elements on the page.
→
Content critique. Dense text and raw statistics were specifically described as "boring" and un-engaging.
→
Design issues. Poor color contrast (e.g., the donation button) and poor mobile scan-ability hindered reading — notable given half our respondents were on mobile.
Two respondents independently called the page "an aimless list of facts" — a small sample, but it echoed exactly what the heuristic evaluation had already flagged structurally. When independent methods point at the same problem, that's a stronger signal than either one alone, even with n=12.Why this finding mattered despite the small sample
Key Insights

What the evidence actually meant for the redesign

01
Numbers meant nothing without visual context.
Evidence: heuristic finding that the page relied on text lists over charts, forcing recall; survey respondents called dense stats "boring."
Design implication: replace dense bulleted number lists with visual card-based treatments that put the number itself front and center.
02
The page was already telling two different stories without connecting them.
Evidence: content analysis found both hard quantitative facts and softer human elements (survey verbatim, faculty quotes) coexisting without any relationship to each other; Community Events — one of the most human, least numeric sections — was independently one of the two most memorable parts of the page.
Design implication: pair quantitative stats with adjacent human quotes throughout the page, not just in isolated sections.
03
A missing perspective created an asymmetry.
Evidence: the existing page had a Faculty POV section but no equivalent Student POV — despite students making up 10 of our 12 survey respondents.
Design implication: add a parallel Student POV section using the same format as Faculty POV.
04
The structure didn't match how people actually think about the library.
Evidence: Locations and Annual Visits existed as two separate, disconnected sections describing the same physical footprint.
Design implication: combine them into one "Campus Footprint" section so location and usage read together.

Rebuilding the Hierarchy

From a flat list to a structure with a reason behind every section

The existing page ran through 10 sections with no clear grouping logic. We restructured around what the insights above actually called for — combining what belonged together, adding what was missing, and giving the highest-impact numbers a dedicated entry point.

Reflection

What this project actually taught the team

✓
What I learned about research. Even a small, quick 12-response survey caught a real, specific failure — that people genuinely couldn't tell what the page was for. Lightweight research still surfaces meaningful problems, even with limited access to the actual target audience within our timeline.
✓
What I learned about stakeholder collaboration. Maggie's framing ("information here and there," needing to serve multiple departments) was the right instinct, but turning that into an actionable design problem required a separate, independent heuristic evaluation. A stakeholder can correctly sense that something's wrong without being able to specify exactly what or why — that gap is the designer's job to close. Her sharing our work with her staff also surfaced a constraint we couldn't have found ourselves: what IT could actually build.
↻
What I'd do differently. Working across different time zones as a 3-person team, combined with limited access to the page's actual external audience, constrained how deep the primary research could go. We're running a follow-up survey against the final redesign this week — in hindsight, building that validation step into the timeline earlier, rather than as a late addition, would have been the stronger plan.
◆
On using AI in this project. AI tools were used for parts of this process and, worth being honest about: they hallucinated at points and needed to be triple-checked, and using them well required real expertise across multiple tools rather than being a shortcut. What they did do well was remove manual busywork and speed up decision-making — useful for ideation, not a substitute for actual design judgment.
