Fandubs: designing safe spaces for isolated students

How might we help socially anxious UW students find peers who share their fandom interests — and ease the transition from anonymous online connection to real-world friendship?

Problem

Students hide their fandom interests in person due to stigma, and rarely bridge online fan connections into real friendships.

Approach

Led 6 user interviews to surface 4 core barriers, then designed a hybrid event + avatar app that eases the online-to-IRL transition.

Impact

Hi-fi prototype built around psychological safety as the primary design driver, not just feature parity with existing social apps.

01 · Discover — The Problem

An unspoken rule was keeping people apart

Students who are passionate about fandoms — TV shows, anime, games — often hide that part of themselves on campus. There's an unspoken rule in fandom culture: don't talk about fandom in public. The fear of judgment is real, and the social cost of being perceived as "weird" in an icebreaker is enough to keep people silent.

At the same time, these same students are finding each other online — in Discord servers and fan forums — but rarely making the leap to real-world friendship. The gap between "we follow each other" and "let's actually hang out" is where people were stuck.

I would never say that in an icebreaker because it would be like — all right. That's not how I want my first impression to go. Like some sort of weirdo.Interview participant 1
It's an assumption about a person, before you even know their story behind what they do.Interview participant 2
02 · Define — Research Findings

Four themes from six interviews

We recruited UW undergrad students who identified as fandom participants. Interviews surfaced four consistent pain point clusters — all of which had to be addressed for any solution to work.

→
Social stigma. Sharing fandom interests in-person felt risky in a way it didn't online. Students actively monitored what they revealed in group settings.
→
Psychological barriers. Social anxiety and fear of judgment were the biggest blockers — not lack of interest. People wanted to connect; they just couldn't get themselves to start.
→
Time constraints. Between commuting, coursework, and part-time jobs, students had real scheduling complexity. A solution needed to work around busy lives, not expect free time.
→
Physical barriers. Meeting strangers in person felt higher-stakes than meeting online. Without a warm intro or shared context, it was hard to bridge "someone I follow" to "someone I know."
How can we make it easier for socially anxious undergraduate UW students to connect with a peer who shares the same fandom, and create a safe space for the transition between online and real-life interaction?Design question
03 · Design — Ideation

Three directions. One key question: what creates the safest on-ramp?

We generated three distinct concepts, each targeting different parts of the problem. The decision came down to which one could lower the barrier to in-person connection without making it feel forced.

ConceptHandles time constraintsReduces anxietyBridges online → IRL
Virtual Avatar SNS AppPartialYesYes — gamified incentive
Proximity Pet DeviceNoYes — subtle designYes — passive discovery
Social Calendar IntegrationYesPartialNo — info only

We moved forward with a hybrid of the Avatar App and Calendar concept — using shared events as the bridge, gamification as the incentive to show up, and interest-based matching to reduce the cold-start awkwardness of messaging a stranger.

Design requirements

What success looks like before a line of UI is drawn:

1
Find events that work with a user's actual time constraints — not hypothetical free time
2
Connect socially isolated students with others who share the same fandom, not just broadly similar tastes
3
Give people a conversation starter — don't leave the first message up to them
4
Ease users into in-person interaction — online connection first, IRL second
5
Make the space feel safe: judgment-free framing, clear community norms, user control over what's visible

From wireframes to hi-fi prototype

Prototyping focused on the two highest-anxiety moments: matching with someone new, and making the step from "chatting in app" to "meeting at an event." The avatar system lets users warm up to each other at a pace they control.

04 · Measure — Reflection

What I learned leading a research-heavy team project

✓
What worked — starting with the emotional barrier, not the feature gap. Most social apps try to solve loneliness by adding more ways to message people. What we found is that messaging wasn't the problem — the courage to start was. Designing for psychological safety first changed everything downstream.
↻
Would do differently — validate the concept earlier with low-fidelity tests. We went deep on research before testing any design ideas. I'd run scrappy concept tests much earlier, even paper prototypes, to fail faster and stay closer to what users actually need.
◆
PM lens — every feature is a tradeoff, even when the need is real. The gamification system was exciting to design but added significant scope. I practiced asking: "does this solve the core barrier, or are we adding complexity that feels good in theory?" That question cut two features.
?
Still learning — how to measure "safe enough to be yourself." The hardest metric to define was psychological safety. I know it matters — the research made that undeniable — but I'm still learning how to translate something that qualitative into design success criteria I can actually test.
