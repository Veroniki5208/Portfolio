Backpack User Research: understanding campus life through everyday objects

Students carry backpacks for hours every day, often filled with laptops, books, water bottles, and personal items. Through initial observations on campus, I noticed wide variations in how students wore and interacted with their backpacks — sometimes in ways that suggested discomfort or inefficiency. This study investigates how UW students carry, organize, and place their backpacks, aiming to inform design improvements.

01 · Discover — Field Observations

Watching how students actually carry, not how they say they do

I wanted to understand how students physically carry backpacks across terrains and movement contexts, what they carry inside vs. outside, how they place backpacks in shared spaces, and how environment (traffic, crowding) shapes those behaviors.

📍
CSE Building (level 0) — movement through stairs and classrooms
📍
Red Square — high traffic, outdoor, varied terrain
📍
U-District Light Rail — sitting, standing, waiting, crowd navigation

Observations were coded into four categories: built environment, possessions, traffic, and overall experience.

What the observations showed

Carrying styles. Students wore backpacks in ways that ranged from correct (both shoulders, straps tightened) to risky: both shoulders with loose straps sitting low on the back, one shoulder only, or skipping chest/hip straps entirely even when available.

Utilization. Despite available backpack space, students often carried items in their hands anyway — extra bags, water bottles, headphones, phones, wallets and keys — signaling accessibility, not capacity, was the real constraint.

Placement. When seated, students kept backpacks on (limiting item access), balanced them on their lap (crowding laptop/notebook space), set them on the floor (clutter, tripping hazard), or on shared tables (occupying communal surface).

02 · Define — Interview Findings

Three 1:1 interviews to understand the "why"

Field observations showed what students did. Semi-structured interviews with 3 students got at why — motivations around comfort, convenience, ergonomics, and how aesthetic preference factors into daily use.

It caused more discomfort when… it felt really heavy and caused shoulder pain.Participant 1
I fully have to take my backpack off, put it at my feet, bend over and grab it — I have to unclasp it and then pull open the zipper.Participant 1, on accessing items
I will always try to figure out how to customize a bag… I will add charms or keychains to it, to the best of my ability.Participant 2

Three themes emerged

→
Poor ergonomic support causes real discomfort. Multiple participants reported shoulder pain after prolonged wear, tied to thin, unpadded straps and poor weight distribution.
→
Backpack design doesn't support quick access. Students compensate for hard-to-reach main compartments by using secondary bags for high-frequency items like phone, keys, and wallet.
→
Backpacks are identity, not just utility. Participants valued customization (pins, charms, keychains) as self-expression — one specifically rated look/style as "60% important" in their choice.
03 · Design — Recommendations

Translating findings into design direction

For each theme, I paired the finding with concrete design and behavioral recommendations — the intended next step before this moves into an actual prototype.

FindingRecommendation
Incorrect carrying causes long-term strainVisual strap-length cues for equal shoulder distribution; ergonomic-carrying education at point of purchase and on campus
Students hand-carry items despite available spaceEasy-reach exterior pockets for phone/keys/wallet; adjustable interior compartments; attachment points for extra bags
Seating placement creates clutter or blocked accessDesignated backpack spaces — hooks on chairs/tables, under-seat shelving; signage to increase shared-space awareness
Shoulder pain from prolonged wearMemory foam / gel padding on straps and back panel; wider straps; fit guidance for different body types
Backpacks as self-expressionProtected zones for pins/patches; built-in strap or hook attachment points; swappable strap or zipper designs
04 · Measure — Survey & Status

Validating at scale

To check whether interview findings held beyond 3 people, I ran a 13-question survey distributed via UW's Instagram story. It stayed live for 24 hours and reached ~1,200 views and 47 responses — covering prevalence of key behaviors (one-shoulder wearing, loose straps, hand-carrying), discomfort levels, and feature priorities.
