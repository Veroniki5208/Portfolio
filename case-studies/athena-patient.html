AthenaPatient: a 4.9-star app hiding real failures

I tried to update my address in the AthenaPatient mobile app. It sent me to the mobile website. The mobile website also didn't have it. I had to switch to desktop. That moment became a full feature parity audit — and a redesign.

Approach

App Store review analysis + competitive audit vs. MyChart + personal usability testing to prioritize a 4-week scope.

01 · Discover — The Problem

I found the bug the rating was hiding

Trying to update my mailing address in the AthenaPatient app, I hit a redirect to the mobile website. The mobile website also didn't support it. I had to open a laptop to change my address — a task that should take 30 seconds in a healthcare app.

That sent me to the App Store to look at 1-star reviews. What I found wasn't random frustration — it was a pattern. The same features kept coming up. The same word kept appearing: "useless." Meanwhile, the aggregate rating sat at 4.9 stars.

A high aggregate rating can actively obscure a broken product. When the majority of users only need appointments (which works), they 5-star it. When a user needs billing or profile management, they hit a wall — and become a 1-star. The average hides both groups.The core insight

Three lenses on the same problem

→
Personal usability testing. Audited every core patient portal task firsthand — appointments, billing, messaging, lab results, profile management — documenting exactly where each one broke or redirected.
→
App Store review analysis. Analyzed 1-star critical reviews to identify complaint patterns at scale. Recent reviews (Jan 2025) consistently described the same redirect failures — confirming the problem wasn't old or resolved.
→
Competitor benchmarking. Compared feature parity against MyChart, the market leader. Every feature AthenaPatient was missing, MyChart had — framing this as a competitive risk, not just a UX inconvenience.
→
User flow mapping. Mapped the actual journey users were taking — app → mobile web → desktop — to make the fragmentation visible as a multi-platform context switch, not just a missing button.
02 · Define — Key Findings

What 1-star reviews actually said

The most recent critical reviews weren't scattered complaints — they clustered tightly around five issues. Each one represents a core healthcare task that should be completable in a patient app.

→
Doctor's visit notes unavailable in-app — redirects to website
→
Billing and payment impossible — redirects to website instead of processing in-app
→
Messaging failures — messages restart mid-send or don't go through at all
→
Lab results and referrals missing — redirected to web for basic clinical info
→
Profile management absent — can't update address, contact info, or account settings in-app

This is a business risk disguised as a UX problem

AthenaPatient's user base is largely captive — patients use the app because their doctor's practice requires it. That creates a false sense of security in the ratings: users who can't switch apps can only complain, not leave. But that dynamic can shift.

🏥
Trust erosion. Healthcare requires trust above almost any other product category. A broken billing feature or redirected lab result signals the company doesn't take the patient's experience seriously.
⚠️
Retention risk. Frustrated patients may pressure their doctors to switch to practices using competitors with better portals — MyChart was specifically named in reviews.
📉
Adoption barrier. New users, often onboarded because a new doctor requires Athena, hit friction immediately. A redirect on day one sets a damaging precedent.
📊
Misleading metrics. The 4.9-star aggregate actively masks the severity of these failures from stakeholders. If product decisions are made from that number, the underlying pain isn't heard.
03 · Design — Prioritization & Scope

Six missing features. Four weeks. Here's how I chose.

I evaluated each missing feature across two dimensions: frequency of need and impact of failure. The highest-impact, highest-frequency failures became the scope.

FeatureStatusWhy
Billing Section✓ Redesigned — Phase 1"Cannot pay in-app" was the single most repeated complaint. Financial info in healthcare is never optional.
Profile Section✓ Redesigned — Phase 1My own experience started here. Not being able to update your address is a fundamental failure.
Visit Notes Viewer→ Deferred — Phase 2Requires complex medical data display and HIPAA-compliant formatting — higher complexity than 4-week scope allows.
Full Messaging Overhaul→ Deferred — Phase 2Failures appear partly technical (messages restarting mid-send). Needs engineering investigation before UI redesign.

What I built

Billing Section — a unified billing hub that keeps users entirely in-app for everything money-related, no redirects: balance overview, in-app payment processing, insurance details, payment history, and Explanation of Benefits with progressive disclosure.

Four key design decisions

1
Consolidated billing into one hub. Reduces cognitive load and matches user mental models — "where do I see everything about money?" has one answer.
2
Separated patient info from billing info in Profile. Users update medical info and payment methods for different reasons at different times — separating them prevents accidental edits.
3
Progressive disclosure for complex data. Summary cards expand to detail views for dense features like Explanation of Benefits — shows the key number first, prevents cognitive overload.
4
Preserved Athena's existing visual language. Maintained the existing design system throughout so new features feel native, not bolted on.

Reflection

What I learned from a self-initiated project

✓
What worked — starting from my own experience made the problem real. The redirect I hit personally gave me an authentic stake in the problem. That motivation is hard to manufacture.
✓
What worked — App Store reviews as qualitative data at scale. Critical reviews gave rich, unprompted feedback without recruiting. The pattern across dozens of 1-star reviews was more convincing than any survey I could have designed.
↻
Would do differently — more structured interviews beyond friends and family. My usability testing was limited to five users in my network. Recruiting actual Athena patients, especially older adults, would have caught edge cases I likely missed.
↻
Would do differently — quantitative data on which redirects users hit most. I prioritized by qualitative signals, not usage data. Real analytics would sharpen prioritization significantly.

Next steps

1
Validate priorities with real Athena patients through structured interviews — especially older adults and low digital-literacy users
2
Design the visit notes viewer — the most requested missing feature in critical reviews, deferred due to scope
3
Investigate messaging failures — determine whether they're frontend or backend before redesigning the UI
4
Test with diverse users — non-English speakers, elderly patients, users with low digital literacy
5
Create a phased rollout plan with clear success metrics for each phase
