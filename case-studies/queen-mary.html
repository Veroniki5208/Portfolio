Queen Mary Tea: fancy but approachable, finally looking the part

One of Seattle's oldest independent tea rooms had a brand that felt as cluttered as their original website — busy backgrounds, mismatched fonts, and a to-go menu that was nearly impossible to read. This project rebuilt the identity from the ground up: new logo, color system, typography, and two redesigned artifacts.

Problem

A "fancy but approachable" brand undercut by visual clutter — competing background patterns, inconsistent type, and no color hierarchy.

Approach

Built a full identity system — palette, typography, logo marks — then applied it to a website page and a physical to-go menu.

Impact

Website information architecture reorganized from unordered to pedagogical sequencing; both artifacts defended in peer + faculty critique.

Context

A beloved Seattle institution with a brand that wasn't keeping up

Queen Mary Tea is one of the oldest independent tea rooms in America — a formal British tea experience with scones, pastries, and custom blended teas. The target audience bridges generations: women who want to bring their grandmothers and granddaughters together over afternoon tea.

The brand positioning was clear — "fancy but approachable" — but the existing visual identity wasn't delivering on either half. It leaned too busy to feel elegant, and too overwhelming to feel welcoming.

Queen Mary's brand identity should communicate a sense of community within traditional and elegant British tea culture — to tea lovers in Seattle and tourists alike.Design intent

Color System

Five colors, each with a job

The palette needed to feel historically grounded without feeling stuffy, and warm enough for the community aspect without losing the elegance. Every color was assigned a specific role before it touched any artifact.

Color, typography, and logo direction were each developed independently by my partner and me, then brought together for a series of peer and professor critiques — examining each other's work, challenging decisions, and refining rationale through multiple rounds of feedback. Being able to explain why each decision was made was a core requirement of the project.

Typography

Three typefaces, one voice

Each typeface was chosen for a specific role — not just aesthetics, but what it communicates to someone glancing at the menu or landing on the website for the first time.

TypefaceRoleWhy
Zen Old MinchoBrand name / logo wordmarkContains a Q that mirrors the Q in the emblem mark — visual cohesion between wordmark and icon
EB GaramondTitles and section headersFormal yet inviting — matches the "fancy but approachable" brief without feeling cold
LailaBody textElegant sans-serif, legible at menu sizes without losing brand character
Identity

Two marks, one system

The logo system uses an emblem mark and a wordmark that combine into a combination mark. The emblem — a crowned Q with a gold teacup at the center — stands alone on physical items like stamps or embossed labels. The combination mark adds the wordmark for contexts where the brand name needs to be explicit.

Digital Redesign — Tea-torial Page

The website wasn't cluttered by accident — it just had no system

The original Tea-torial page was trying to do too much at once: multiple background patterns competed with text, fonts changed sizes without reason, and the content order followed no clear logic. A user trying to learn about different types of tea would have to fight the page to do it. The core problem wasn't any single element — it was the absence of visual hierarchy.

Reorganizing the information architecture

The original Tea-torial content had no clear order — types of tea and topics were interleaved with no logic. The redesign groups content by category, starting with foundational concepts (afternoon tea vs. high tea, food terms, caffeine and weights) before moving into specific tea types — mimicking how someone would actually be taught about British tea culture.

The original nav bar was also cluttered and made finding reservations difficult, so "Visit" became a dedicated nav item with the Tea-torial page nested underneath it — letting visitors know what they're getting into before they dive in.

Analog Redesign — To-Go Menu

Everything was cramped together

The original to-go menu packed tea listings against a busy decorative background, making it difficult to read and overwhelming at a glance. The redesign simplifies the background and spaces out the text, keeping the same tea cup motif for brand continuity while dramatically improving readability.

Key Design Decisions

Four choices that defined the whole system

1
Solid backgrounds over patterns everywhere. The original leaned on heavy floral patterns as the primary surface. The redesign uses solid cream (#F7F1E3) as the default background with pattern as a subtle accent only — because readable content matters more than decorative texture.
2
Purple and gold reserved for hierarchy signals, not decoration. In the original, both colors appeared everywhere without consistent meaning. In the redesign, purple means "section header or primary brand element" and gold means "accent or interactive element."
3
Tea-torial IA reorganized from random to pedagogical. The redesign starts with concepts before getting into tea types — content that educates should be structured like education.
4
Combination mark for digital, emblem mark for physical. The emblem stands alone well at small sizes — a stamp, an embossed label, a favicon. The combination mark works where establishing the brand name matters.
Reflection

What I learned doing a rebrand with a partner

✓
What worked — defining color roles before touching any artifact. Assigning each color a job before designing saved time and prevented the inconsistency that hurt the original. When purple always means "primary brand," decisions become automatic instead of arbitrary.
✓
What worked — treating the IA as a UX problem, not just organization. Reordering the Tea-torial content wasn't a minor cleanup — it was a decision about how people learn, which gave me a clear rationale to defend in critique.
↻
Would do differently — user testing before finalizing the IA. The content reordering was based on my own logic about how someone learns about tea. I'd want to card-sort this with actual users, especially people unfamiliar with British tea, to validate the sequence.
↻
Would do differently — more menu iterations with print-specific constraints. Digital and print have very different readability requirements. Something that looks clean on screen can still be hard to read on paper — I'd test the printed menu at actual size earlier.
